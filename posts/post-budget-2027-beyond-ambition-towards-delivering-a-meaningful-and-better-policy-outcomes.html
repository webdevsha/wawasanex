Post Budget 2027: Beyond Ambition, Towards Delivering a Meaningful and Better Policy Outcomes

Adli Amirullah

Executive Summary

Budget 2027 introduces several important measures to strengthen Malaysia's digital economy, support local businesses and prepare for an ageing society. While these initiatives are welcomed, their success should not be measured solely by government allocations, targets or new regulatory frameworks. Building on these announcements, Wawasanex puts forward supplementary policy recommendations across three key areas, with the aim of strengthening implementation and maximising their potential economic and social benefits for Malaysians.

First, Malaysia's AI ambition must go beyond developing 200,000 skilled workers and establishing governance guidelines. With online scam losses reaching RM2.97 billion in 2025, Wawasanex recommends strengthening public AI awareness through real-world fraud education while promoting applied AI projects that address practical challenges such as flood prediction, healthcare efficiency and agricultural productivity. Second, the proposed E-Commerce Bill should protect SMEs against unfair practices without unnecessarily restricting consumer choice. If restrictions on foreign e-commerce platforms are introduced, Wawasanex proposes a transition period of no more than five years, allowing SMEs to strengthen their competitiveness through targeted government assistance before the market is fully reopened. Third, Budget 2027's allocation of over RM40 million to train 8,000 aged-care workers must translate into sustainable employment opportunities. Wawasanex recommends strengthening private-sector digital care platforms and professional intermediaries to better connect households with qualified caregivers, improve access to professional care and create more consistent employment opportunities.

Ultimately, these recommendations aim to complement the government's existing initiatives by encouraging greater public-private collaboration, strengthening market efficiency and ensuring that Budget 2027 delivers meaningful improvements in productivity, household welfare and Malaysia's long-term economic competitiveness.

Policy Cluster 1: Malaysia’s AI Ambition Must Go Beyond Skilled Workers and Guidelines

Budget 2027’s allocation of nearly RM15 million to AI Malaysia Berhad, alongside the ambition to develop 200,000 AI-skilled workers, is a welcome development. However, even if Malaysia successfully achieves these targets, what does success actually look like for ordinary Malaysians? Most people may never work directly in AI, but they will increasingly interact with the technology in their daily lives, bringing both opportunities and risks. The growing sophistication of AI-enabled fraud is one example. According to PDRM, online scam losses reached RM2.97 billion in 2025, involving more than 66,000 cases, while MCMC secured the removal of over 12,000 deepfake-related posts in the first six months of 2026. Although these scams cannot be entirely attributed to AI, synthetic media introduces another layer of risk to an already serious problem. Malaysia already has initiatives such as the National Scam Response Centre, while Budget 2027 proposes an AI application to verify suspicious financial information. The challenge now is ensuring that Malaysians are equipped to recognise these emerging threats before becoming victims, rather than relying primarily on enforcement after harm has occurred.

At the same time, Malaysia should place greater emphasis on applied AI that directly addresses economic and social challenges faced by the rakyat. For instance, AI could strengthen flood prediction and early warning systems, reduce waiting times in public hospitals, help paddy farmers improve crop yields, or address traffic congestion and river pollution in the Klang Valley. These are practical applications that could improve productivity, household welfare and the efficiency of public spending. If Malaysia is serious about becoming an AI nation by 2030, the focus should not be limited to how many skilled workers we produce or how quickly businesses adopt AI, but also whether the technology creates meaningful improvements in people's daily lives. This requires policymakers to look at both sides of AI development: enabling its productive use while ensuring society is protected against misuse. Ultimately, Malaysia's AI ambition should be measured by the economic and social value it creates for the rakyat, not merely by the targets and investments announced.

Recommendation 1: Strengthen Public AI Awareness and Promote Practical AI Applications

Wawasanex recommends that the government strengthen Malaysia's AI agenda through two complementary initiatives focusing on public education and practical AI adoption. First, a comprehensive AI awareness and education programme should be developed in collaboration with relevant stakeholders, including PDRM, MCMC, Bank Negara Malaysia, educational institutions, financial institutions, digital platforms and content creators. Rather than relying solely on general awareness campaigns, the programme should incorporate real-world case studies, particularly involving AI-generated scams, deepfake impersonation and fraudulent investment schemes. These cases could help Malaysians understand how victims were deceived, recognise warning signs and learn practical ways to protect themselves. Public education should also introduce responsible AI applications that Malaysians can benefit from in their daily lives, including education, employment and business activities.

Second, the government should place greater emphasis on supporting applied AI projects that address specific economic and social challenges faced by the rakyat. This could involve collaboration between relevant ministries, government agencies, universities and technology companies to develop targeted pilot projects in areas such as flood prediction and early warning systems, hospital waiting times, agricultural productivity, traffic congestion and environmental monitoring. Each initiative should have measurable objectives, with its effectiveness assessed through improvements in public service delivery, productivity, cost savings and household welfare before being expanded nationwide. Ultimately, Malaysia's AI ambition should not be measured merely by the number of skilled workers trained or initiatives introduced, but by whether Malaysians are better equipped to use AI safely and whether the technology delivers meaningful economic and social benefits.

Policy Cluster 2: Malaysia Must Protect SMEs, Not Protect Them From Competition

Budget 2027’s proposal to introduce a new E-Commerce Bill is a welcome development, particularly as foreign platforms and cross-border sellers continue to reshape Malaysia’s digital marketplace. There is certainly a need to address unfair trading practices and strengthen consumer protection, but policymakers must distinguish between protecting Malaysian SMEs from unfair competition and protecting them from competition altogether. Cheaper imported products may challenge traditional business models, but they have also improved purchasing power, especially for lower-income Malaysians who can now afford products previously beyond their financial reach. Low prices alone do not constitute unfair competition when they result from efficiency, economies of scale or better supply chains. The government should address product safety and consumer protection without unnecessarily restricting competition that benefits consumers.

Perhaps a more uncomfortable question is whether Malaysia has been too focused on preserving SMEs rather than improving their productivity. According to DOSM, MSMEs contributed 39.7% of GDP and accounted for 48.7% of national employment in 2025. Yet, average monthly wages in MSMEs stood at RM2,810 in 2022, compared with RM4,145 in large enterprises. Anecdotally, I have encountered businesses where owners could afford European luxury cars while experienced executives earned merely RM2,000 to RM3,000 monthly. Of course, this does not represent every SME, but it raises a legitimate question about whether government protection or assistance sufficiently translates into better employment outcomes. Malaysia should aspire to develop businesses that innovate, create their own products and compete internationally, rather than depending indefinitely on importing cheap goods for resale without meaningful differentiation. Protecting uncompetitive business models may preserve existing enterprises, but it does little to improve long-term productivity or wages.

Recommendation 2: Introduce a 5-Year E-Commerce Transition Framework to Strengthen SME Competitiveness

Wawasanex recommends that the proposed E-Commerce Bill adopt a time-bound approach to any restrictions on direct consumer access to foreign e-commerce platforms, rather than imposing an indefinite ban. While temporary protection may provide Malaysian SMEs with breathing room to adjust to growing foreign competition, permanently restricting these platforms could undermine consumer purchasing power and limit access to affordable products, particularly for lower-income households. If such restrictions are introduced, the government should establish a clear transition timeline of no more than five years, with a sunset clause and periodic reviews to assess their impact on both businesses and consumers. The restrictions should be proportionate, legally justified and progressively relaxed where appropriate, rather than automatically maintained throughout the entire period.

More importantly, this transition period must be accompanied by targeted government support to help SMEs improve productivity, adopt technology, develop their own products and strengthen their competitiveness. Assistance should be linked to measurable improvements in innovation, employee remuneration and business capabilities, rather than merely sustaining existing operations. Malaysia should not encourage business models that depend indefinitely on importing inexpensive products for domestic resale without meaningful value creation. By establishing a clear timeline for opening the market to direct foreign competition, the government would give SMEs an incentive to innovate while providing consumers with certainty that restrictions will not become permanent. Ultimately, the purpose of temporary protection should be to develop stronger Malaysian businesses capable of competing in an open marketplace, not to shield them from competition indefinitely.

Policy Cluster 3: Malaysia Must Turn Care Needs into Sustainable Careers

Budget 2027 deserves recognition for introducing measures to strengthen Malaysia’s care economy, including the proposed Senior Citizens Bill, expanded caregiving tax relief, lower service taxes on elderly-care services and more than RM40 million through HRD Corp to train 8,000 aged-care workers. These initiatives signal growing recognition that caregiving should be treated as an economic activity rather than solely a family responsibility. However, training 8,000 caregivers raises another important question. What happens after they complete their training? Who will employ them, how much will they earn, and will caregiving offer meaningful career progression? Increasing the supply of trained caregivers alone will not necessarily create a sustainable care industry if employment opportunities remain limited or unattractive.

The challenge is not simply a shortage of trained caregivers, but also how Malaysia organises demand for professional care services. Many families require childcare or elderly-care support but struggle to identify reliable providers, while qualified caregivers may face irregular bookings and uncertain incomes. This fragmented market creates an opportunity for digital care platforms and professional intermediaries to improve how families and caregivers find each other, coordinate services and establish longer-term care arrangements. Platforms such as Kiddocare illustrate the potential of technology to reduce market inefficiencies and improve access to care services. However, better matching does not automatically guarantee higher wages or employment security, particularly when household affordability remains a constraint. Malaysia should therefore look beyond training targets and consider how private-sector innovation, appropriate regulation and targeted financial support can work together to build a more efficient care market. Ultimately, the success of Budget 2027's care-economy initiatives should be measured not merely by how many caregivers are trained, but by whether Malaysia can create a sustainable industry where professional care is accessible to families and caregiving offers stable, rewarding career opportunities.

Recommendation 3: Strengthen the Role of Digital Care Platforms in Developing a Sustainable Care Economy

Wawasanex proposes that the government strengthen the role of private-sector care platforms and professional intermediaries in developing Malaysia’s care economy. While Budget 2027’s allocation of more than RM40 million to train 8,000 aged-care workers is commendable, increasing the supply of trained caregivers alone will not guarantee sustainable employment. The government should also focus on creating a more efficient care market where families can access qualified caregivers while workers benefit from more consistent employment opportunities.

Digital care platforms does demonstrate how technology can help connect fragmented household demand with available caregivers, reduce matching costs and improve workforce utilisation. Rather than developing new government-operated platforms, policymakers should encourage accredited private-sector intermediaries to expand their services through appropriate regulatory support, industry partnerships and incentives for recurring care arrangements. Targeted financial assistance could also help lower-income households access professional care through qualified providers, strengthening demand across the sector.

However, greater market efficiency must translate into better outcomes for caregivers and care recipients. Participating platforms should meet clear standards for worker remuneration, professional qualifications, social protection and service quality. Ultimately, Malaysia should move beyond simply training more caregivers towards building a well-functioning care market that makes professional services affordable for families while creating more stable and rewarding employment opportunities.

Conclusion

Budget 2027 reflects the government's continued commitment to strengthening Malaysia's economic foundation while preparing for emerging challenges in technology, business competitiveness and demographic change. The initiatives announced provide an important starting point, and there are opportunities to further amplify their impact through complementary policies that encourage innovation, strengthen market participation and improve the effectiveness of implementation.

Across the three policy clusters, the challenges may be different, but the underlying policy lesson remains relevant. Malaysia's AI development should go beyond producing skilled workers towards protecting the rakyat against emerging risks and encouraging practical applications that improve public services and productivity. Supporting SMEs should focus on building businesses capable of competing through innovation and efficiency, rather than relying indefinitely on market protection at the expense of consumers. Similarly, expanding caregiver training must be accompanied by efforts to strengthen the care ecosystem, particularly by enabling private-sector intermediaries to connect families with qualified caregivers while improving employment opportunities and service accessibility.

Ultimately, effective policymaking requires more than having the right economic vision. It requires the policy instinct to understand how markets operate, recognise unintended consequences and determine when government intervention is necessary or when the private sector should be allowed to play a greater role. The real test of Budget 2027 is not how much the government spends or how many initiatives it introduces, but whether those interventions create lasting improvements in productivity, household welfare and Malaysia's economic competitiveness.
